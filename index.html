The Craft of Motion
Sep 02, 2026 · Process

Motion design is not merely about making things move. It is about giving meaning to movement, creating a visual language that communicates intent before a single word is read.

At Article Motion, every project begins with a question: what should the audience feel? From there, we build systems of motion — easing curves, timing ratios, spatial relationships — that guide the viewer through an emotional arc.

The best motion work is invisible. It does not announce itself; it simply makes the experience feel right. This is the standard we hold ourselves to on every frame.

Behind the Reel
Aug 15, 2026 · Studio

Our 2024 showreel represents six months of curated work, distilled into ninety seconds. The selection process was brutal — favorites were cut, experiments were shelved, and only the work that best represented our range made the final cut.

What remains is a portrait of the studio: diverse in style, consistent in quality, and unafraid to experiment. From photorealistic product renders to abstract motion studies, the reel demonstrates that animation is a medium, not a genre.

Color in Animation
Jul 28, 2026 · Design

Color is the first thing an audience processes, and the last thing they remember. In motion design, color does not merely decorate — it directs attention, establishes hierarchy, and encodes emotion.

We approach color with the same rigor as typography or composition. Every palette is tested against the brand, the medium, and the cultural context. A color that works on a dark cinema screen may fail on a bright mobile display.

The most successful projects are those where color becomes a character in its own right, moving and breathing with the animation.

Client Collaboration
Jul 10, 2026 · Process

The best client relationships are partnerships. We do not execute briefs — we interrogate them, expand them, and sometimes challenge them. The goal is not to deliver what was asked, but to deliver what was needed.

Our process is built on transparency. Clients see work in progress, not just finished frames. This requires trust, but it produces better results. When a client understands why a decision was made, they can provide feedback that improves the work rather than diluting it.

Tools We Use
Jun 22, 2026 · Tech

Our pipeline is tool-agnostic. We use After Effects for motion graphics, Cinema 4D for 3D, and Houdini for simulations. But the tool is never the point — the idea is.

What matters is fluency. A designer who knows one tool deeply will outperform one who knows many superficially. We invest in deep expertise, then connect those experts into teams that can tackle any challenge.

Sound Design
Jun 05, 2026 · Audio

Sound is half the experience. A beautifully animated sequence with poor audio feels cheap; a simple animation with great sound feels expensive. We treat audio as a design discipline, not an afterthought.

Our sound design process runs parallel to visual development. We sketch audio concepts early, refine them alongside the visuals, and mix them with the same attention to detail we apply to every frame.

A letter from Neil deGrasse Tyson, the astrophysicist, replied to Ronald who was suffering from epilepsy but aspired to be an astronaut. He was often ridiculed by his classmates. The astrophysicist encouraged Ronald to keep his passion, find like-minded friends and wish him a bright future.

The Letter

Dear Ronald,

Thank you for candidly sharing your struggles, and thank you too, for your passion for space.

In my own circles, "Space Intern" is a title worn with pride, and being a "Tech Geek" is nothing less than a badge of honor. Don't forget—the world's wealthiest man, Bill Gates, is a self-proclaimed tech geek. So is NASA Administrator Michael Griffin. And so am I. So, when your classmates tease you about your love for space, just remember that there are thousands of people just like you all over the world—and we understand you. Furthermore, never forget that it is only those who possess true passion and unwavering persistence who go on to achieve great things.

As for your occasional seizures—yes, they may indeed prevent you from becoming an astronaut. But ultimately, epilepsy is no different from many other common medical conditions. You may need to take prescription medication over the long term to keep it under control, but it will not diminish your intelligence, nor will it stop you from becoming a mathematician, an engineer, or a scientist. You could even design aircraft and spacecraft; even if you cannot personally venture into space, your work could still stand at the very forefront of scientific discovery.

Please remember: behind every single astronaut in space, there stand thousands of scientists and engineers.

Based on your return address, I noticed that you live in the Rocky Mountain region. Colorado Springs happens to be the headquarters of the Space Foundation—an organization that runs a great many programs—and I strongly encourage you to pay them a visit. If you are willing to make the trip, I'd bet you'll return home with a whole box full of cool goodies: pens, posters, pins, paperweights, and other items perfect for science fairs. I can say this with such confidence because I serve on the Space Foundation's Board of Directors, and every time I visit their headquarters, I bring back a box full of cool stuff myself!

If you do go, you are sure to meet kindred spirits at the Space Foundation headquarters—people who couldn't care less about the snide remarks of foolish classmates—and you will share a wonderful time together, however brief that time may be.

Sending you my very best wishes—from here on Earth, and across the universe.

Neil deGrasse Tyson
